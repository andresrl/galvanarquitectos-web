A well-made image is immediately convincing. That is why it helps to know what you are looking at when you receive a proposal: a **photograph of a built space**, or a **render**, an image produced from a digital model of the design.

Both are useful. The problem starts when they are read in the same way: a photograph documents something that exists; a render represents an intention that may still change.

## What a render is, and what it is for

A render lets you see a space before it is built. It helps you understand proportion, the relationship between rooms, the way light enters and the character of materials. In a design conversation, it can be the quickest way to check whether an idea is heading where you expect.

But a render is a representation. Whoever prepares it chooses the viewpoint, the time of day, the lighting, and the furniture and planting that appear. All of that shapes how you feel when you look at it.

## What an image can exaggerate without meaning to

When reviewing a render, look at these points before drawing conclusions:

- **The viewpoint.** A very wide camera can make a room look larger than it will feel when you are standing in it.
- **The light.** A dusk scene, or one with every light on, conveys a particular atmosphere. Ask how the space will look at other times of day.
- **The materials.** On screen, a finish can look lighter, glossier or more even than the real sample.
- **Furniture and styling.** Sofas, rugs, plants and objects help you read the scale, but they are not necessarily part of the commission.

None of this makes a render misleading. It is simply a tool that needs context.

## Useful questions about a proposal

When you receive images of a project, you can ask:

1. Is this image a render or a photograph?
2. Which decisions does it reflect, and which are still open?
3. Have the materials shown been chosen, or are they a reference?
4. Is the furniture part of the scope, or does it only help to read the scale?
5. Are there drawings or samples that go with this image?

The last question matters. A render is easier to understand alongside the drawings that define dimensions and layout, and with material samples when the project includes them.

## Project photographs need context too

Photographs of completed work are a good way to get to know a studio. Even so, ask which part of the project was the architect's work and what the commission covered. A picture of a living room does not, on its own, tell you whether the studio designed the architecture, the interiors or both.

A studio archive may hold both photographs and renders. If the difference matters to you when assessing a project, ask directly.

## Making images work for you

Images work best as the starting point of a conversation rather than as a final result. If you like something, explain what it is: the light, the proportion, the connection with the outdoors. If something does not convince you, try to pin down why.

That information helps refine the design far more than a general yes or no.
