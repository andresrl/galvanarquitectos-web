When you review a set of **architectural drawings**, you do not need to understand every symbol on first reading. You can start by identifying what each drawing shows, walking through the spaces in your mind and noting what you would like explained.

This guide suggests a way to prepare your questions. It does not describe the documentation included in any particular commission: that scope should be agreed with the studio.

## 1. Identify the document in front of you

Before looking at the layout, find the drawing title, its date and, if shown, the revision reference. If you have several documents, ask which one you should use for your review.

It is also worth clarifying what the drawing represents: an existing situation, a proposal or an alternative still being considered. Do not judge the status of the project only by how finished the drawing looks.

A useful first question is: **"Which decisions does this drawing record, and which are still open?"**

## 2. Tell plans, elevations and sections apart

If the documentation includes these types of drawing, a basic distinction will help you find your way:

- **Plan:** shows how the space is organised, as a horizontal cut. It helps you locate rooms, entrances and the relationships between areas.
- **Elevation:** a vertical view of a façade or an interior wall.
- **Section:** a vertical cut, useful for understanding heights, levels and how spaces relate to one another.

Each drawing offers a different reading. If an area is unclear in plan, ask whether another view would help. You do not need to answer every question from a single sheet.

## 3. Check the dimensions and the legend

Dimensions give measurements. Before interpreting a figure, check the units and the points between which it is measured. If you are not sure whether a dimension refers to the interior of a room or includes other elements, note it as a question.

Do not take measurements from a screenshot as if it necessarily kept the scale of the original. For a dimension that matters to you, ask for the corresponding figure to be identified or clarified.

Look at the legend too, if there is one. **Do not give a fixed meaning to a colour, a line or a hatch pattern** without checking how it is used on that drawing.

## 4. Walk through the house with your routines in mind

Once you have found the entrance, follow an everyday route: arriving, putting your things down, moving to the living area, reaching the bedroom. Then repeat the exercise with other situations that matter to you.

You can ask yourself:

- Do I understand how each room is reached?
- Which areas do I pass through to reach others?
- Does the relationship between spaces match my preferences for privacy?
- Where would the activities I described to the studio take place?
- Which connection with the outdoors do I need to understand better?

These questions do not replace the technical review. They help you share how you expect to live in the house and point out what you would like to discuss.

## 5. Do not confuse what is drawn with what is included

If furniture, planting or other decorative elements appear, ask whether they are indicative or reflect specific decisions. Their presence on the drawing should not lead you to assume they are supplied or part of the agreed scope.

Do the same with finishes: ask what has been defined and what remains to be chosen. Reading the drawing and clarifying the commission are related tasks, but they are not the same.

## 6. Prepare a list of questions you can locate

Rather than writing "I don't understand the living room", try to point to the area and explain what you need to know. For example:

- "At the entrance, what does this line represent?"
- "In the bedroom, between which points is this dimension measured?"
- "In the living area, is the furniture a layout reference?"
- "At this connection with the garden, is there a change of level?"

Group your notes under three headings: reading questions, needs of use and possible changes. You can number them on a copy and keep the original as a reference.
